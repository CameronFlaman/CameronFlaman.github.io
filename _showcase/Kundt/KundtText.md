    <p class="card-text">
      In my experimental methods course, the final project undertaken by my team saw the construction of a serious apparatus to perform some silly science. The Kundt's tube employs a moveable speaker inside cylinder plugged on one end by a material of interest, to create resonance peaks and troughs. A mobile microphone probes along the length of the tube, recording the position and intensity of sound, which can then be used to calculate the sonic absorbance of whatever material is plugging the tube's end. We figured, we might as well measure the absorbance of something nobody else has before, and upon a walk-home after a long day of work, we decided on... charcuterie of course. To relate obtained data to a well-documented result to testing the effectiveness of our setup, we used wood, representing the cutting board itself.
    </p>
    <p>
      The setup consisted of a the tube itself, a 6 foot length of pac with a 3D printed probe with independently-moving microphone and speaker. Bologne, wood, bread and cheddar cheese was scut into perfect cylinder to close the far-end of the tube. The relative absorbances of each charcuterie item in accordance to our predictions, though the accuracy of the wood control to other literature was inconsistent. In conclusion, this setup adequately measures relative absorbances, but has too many sources of error to absolutely conclude the absorbance of a material. And I will never eat bologna again.
    </p>
