    <p class="card-text"> In my experimental methods course, the final project undertaken by my team involved the construction of a serious apparatus to perform some silly science. The Kundt's tube employs a movable speaker inside a cylinder, plugged at one end by a material of interest, to create resonance peaks and troughs. A mobile microphone probes along the length of the tube, recording the position and intensity of sound, which can then be used to calculate the sonic absorbance of whatever material is plugging the tube's end. We figured we might as well measure the absorbance of something nobody else had before, and upon a walk home after a long day of work, we decided on... charcuterie, of course. To relate the obtained data to a well-documented result and test the effectiveness of our setup, we used wood, representing the cutting board itself. 
    </p> 
    <p> The setup consisted of the tube itself, a 6-foot length of PVC, with a 3D-printed probe containing independently moving microphone and speaker. Bologna, wood, bread, and cheddar cheese were cut into perfect cylinders to close the far end of the tube. The relative absorbances of each charcuterie item were in accordance with our predictions, though the accuracy of the wood control compared to other literature was inconsistent. In conclusion, this setup adequately measures relative absorbances, but has too many sources of error to conclusively determine the absolute absorbance of a material. And I will never eat bologna again. 
    </p>
